Black walnuts are everywhere in Champaign County: fence rows, woodlots, yards. Most of them are wild seedlings, and their nuts are mostly shell. In 2025 the University of Missouri Center for Agroforestry patented **UMCA® 'Hickman'**, the first black walnut cultivar patented specifically for **nut (kernel) production** rather than timber. It bears young, bears heavy, and gives you about three times the nutmeat per nut of a wild tree.

This post covers the history, the zones, and the benefits, then gets practical: **when** to order and plant, **how** to get trees here, and **who** to contact. It ends with the current sources and a repeatable process for finding new ones as more nurseries get licensed.

## History

- **2002:** Dr. Mark Coggeshall, then leading the Mizzou Black Walnut Improvement Program, made a controlled cross at the Horticulture and Agroforestry Research Farm (HARF) in New Franklin, Missouri. The mother was **'Sparks 127'** and the father **'Football'**. One seedling from that cross, tree number 02.1055, became Hickman. DNA markers later confirmed the parentage.
- **Early on:** It was the highest-yielding of the 120 seedlings in that 2002 generation and the first to bear nuts, at five years old.
- **2019:** Dr. Ron Revord took over the program from Coggeshall and carried Hickman through release.
- **January 14, 2025:** U.S. Plant Patent **PP36382** was issued to the Curators of the University of Missouri. MU announced it in spring 2025.
- **The name:** It comes from the Hickman House, an 1819 brick home on the HARF farm that's listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Revord compared the house, a milestone in settling the Missouri River hills, to what this cultivar means for the program.

The point of the program is to turn black walnut from a wild-gathered crop into a real orchard crop. Hundreds of black walnut cultivars have been named over the past century, but no commercial orchard industry ever took hold. Hammons Products in Stockton, Missouri, the country's main black walnut sheller, still buys most of its nuts from people who pick them up off the ground.

## Zones

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Species range (black walnut) | Zones 4–9 |
| Hickman, per Forrest Keeling's catalog | Zones 4–9 |
| Where it was tested | New Franklin, Missouri only (patent trials) |
| Urbana, OH | Zone 6a (2012 map); about half of Ohio moved to 6b on the 2023 map, so check your ZIP at [planthardiness.ars.usda.gov](https://planthardiness.ars.usda.gov/) |
| Urbana frost dates | Last frost about April 11–20, first frost about October 11–20 |

**Bottom line for Urbana:** Cold hardiness isn't a concern. We're comfortably inside the range, and black walnut is native here. The open question is performance. Hickman has only been trialed in central Missouri, which has deep, well-drained loess soils. Our wetter, more humid summers will test its weak spot (anthracnose, below). Treat a planting here as a small trial, not a sure thing.

## Benefits

| Trait | Hickman | Wild black walnut |
|---|---|---|
| Years to first crop | About 5 | 10–15 |
| Kernel percentage | About 30–36%, highest in wet years | 6–14% |
| Bearing habit | Heavy **spur bearing** (nuts on short side spurs throughout the canopy) | Mostly on shoot tips |
| Nut size | Medium, about 16 g per nut, kernel about 5.1 g | Variable |
| Kernel color | Light, if hulled promptly | Often dark |
| Mature yield | Comparable to 'Sparrow' and 'Neel', higher than 'Football' | Low and erratic |

Why spur bearing matters: walnuts normally bear only at branch tips, so the crop is limited by how many tips the tree has. Spurs put nut-bearing wood all through the canopy, so the tree bears earlier and heavier.

**Economics, for the record.** MU's 2025 annual report cites Hammons estimates. Orchards of improved cultivars could gross $1,000–$2,400 per acre as the trees mature (years 5–9). Yields could pass 4,000 lb in-shell per acre from about year ten. Hammons pays $0.50–$1.20/lb in-shell for improved nuts, compared with about $0.16/lb for wild ones. Those are Missouri orchard numbers under Hammons's improved-nut program, not something a few trees in Urbana will earn. For a backyard or a few acres, the real benefit is a lot more nutmeat for a lot less cracking.

**Weak points to plan for:**

- **Anthracnose:** The patent lists low tolerance to this leaf disease, which likely drives its **alternate bearing** (big crop one year, light the next). Good management (sanitation, spacing for airflow, fertility) reduces this.
- **Pollination:** Hickman is **protogynous**. Its female flowers open (about early May in Missouri) *before* its own pollen sheds (about a week later). It needs pollen from another walnut that sheds earlier. In Champaign County, wild black walnuts within a few hundred yards will usually cover this. For a deliberate orchard, plant a second, **protandrous** cultivar (one that sheds pollen first). MU Extension's guide XM1001 lists bloom type and dates for more than 40 cultivars.
- **Juglone:** It's still a black walnut. Keep it well away from the vegetable garden, tomatoes, and other sensitive plants.

## When, How, Who: Getting Trees to Urbana

### When

| Timing | Step |
|---|---|
| **Now through January** | Contact the nursery and reserve trees. Supply is small and grafted walnut sells out. |
| **Winter** | Pick and prepare the site: full sun, deep and well-drained soil, no standing water, away from the garden. Get a soil test through OSU Extension. |
| **Early to mid April** | Plant container trees once the ground can be worked, around Urbana's last-frost window. Walnuts leaf out late, so don't panic if they look dead into May. |
| **Alternative: October** | Fall planting of container stock also works if spring supply runs out. |
| **Years 1–4** | Water through dry spells, control grass and weeds in a 3–4 ft circle, protect trunks from deer and mowers, train to an open orchard form. |
| **About year 5** | First nuts. Hull promptly to keep kernels light. |

### How

1. **Call Forrest Keeling Nursery** (Elsberry, MO, 800-356-2401, [fknursery.com](https://fknursery.com/)). They're the first licensed grower. Their 2025–26 wholesale catalog lists **Juglans nigra 'UMCA® Hickman' Grafted** in an S3-3 RPM container at **$40.00**, Zones 4–9. When I checked their online retail shop in November 2026, it didn't list Hickman, so **call** and ask about retail orders, minimums, and spring 2027 availability.
2. **Choose delivery:**
   - **Ship it.** Forrest Keeling ships to the lower 48 from its central hub. Ask about box and freight fees, which their volume discounts don't cover.
   - **Pick it up.** Elsberry is north of St. Louis, roughly a 7-hour drive from Urbana. That makes sense if you're buying several trees or adding other native stock. A pickup bed carries 3-gallon pots easily.
3. **Order at least two trees** if there are no wild walnuts nearby, or one Hickman plus a protandrous pollinizer. Forrest Keeling also lists grafted 'Emma K', 'Quick Crop', 'Sauber' and 'Sparrow' at the same price.
4. **Check the tag** when the trees arrive. It should say **UMCA® Hickman**, be grafted (look for the graft union near the base), and ideally carry the patent number PP36382.
5. **Plant** at the same depth as in the pot, with the graft union above the soil line. Space orchard trees widely (MU's orchard guides use wide spacing so light reaches the lower limbs). Mulch, water, and stake against wind for the first season.

> **Patent note:** Hickman is a patented plant. You can grow and enjoy your trees, but you can't graft or propagate them for sale or to give away without a license from MU. That's also why a scion swap isn't a way to get it.

## A Process for Finding Other Sources

Run this every fall (October–December) before ordering season:

1. **Ask the owner.** Email blackwalnut@missouri.edu and ask: "Which nurseries are currently licensed to sell UMCA® Hickman, and which ship to Ohio?" This is the only authoritative list.
2. **Check MU's pages.** Look at the Center for Agroforestry news page and annual report ([centerforagroforestry.org](https://centerforagroforestry.org/)) for new licensee announcements and any new cultivar releases. The program says more cultivars are coming.
3. **Search the exact trade name.** Search `"UMCA Hickman"` and `"Hickman" Juglans nigra grafted`. Licensed nurseries have to use the trademarked name, so plain "Hickman walnut" ads without it are suspect.
4. **Check the nut-tree specialists' new catalogs.** Grafted black walnut is a niche, so licensees are likely to be nut-tree nurseries. Read their winter catalogs and ask them directly whether they've licensed Hickman. Examples include nurseries in Kentucky, Indiana, Ontario and the Pacific Northwest that already sell grafted walnuts. *I haven't confirmed any of them carry Hickman.*
5. **Ask the growers.** Post the question to the Ohio Nut Growers Association and the Northern Nut Growers Association. Members often know about new sources before they're advertised.
6. **Verify before you buy.** Make sure the seller is on MU's licensee list, the trees are **grafted** (not seedlings), and the tag reads UMCA® Hickman. **A "Hickman seedling" is not Hickman.** Nuts from a Hickman tree grow into new, unpredictable trees, not copies.
7. **Have a fallback.** If no Hickman is available in a given year, plant proven grafted nut cultivars (Forrest Keeling lists Emma K, Quick Crop, Sauber and Sparrow) and add Hickman later. Mixed cultivars also help with pollination.
